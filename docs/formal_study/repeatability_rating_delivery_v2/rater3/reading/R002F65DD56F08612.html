## 1. Objective Findings

**Analysis cycle definition.** The released paretic-side cycle runs from an initial contact of that limb (0%) to the next initial contact of the same limb (100%). These endpoints are two successive occurrences of the same event, not a single instant. No intermediate event — including toe-off — is supplied. All timing below is therefore expressed only as a percentage of the analysis cycle; no stance/swing division, sub-phase label, or conventional 60% split is applied.

**Spatiotemporal values as supplied.**
- Gait speed 0.32 m/s. The label "household ambulator (<0.4 m/s)" denotes a speed band only; it does not describe measured walking independence, safety, or real-world community mobility.
- Cadence 94.2 steps/min.
- Step length symmetry index −11.3%, computed under the **successive-footfall** definition (each heel at its own initial contact). By the stated sign convention, the negative value means the **paretic step is the shorter of the two**. The alternative instantaneous definition can yield a different value in some subjects; only the successive-footfall value is supplied here, so the magnitude should not be treated as definition-independent.

**Reference-comparison basis and its limits.** The deviation reference actually used for this subject is a **pooled cohort of 138 healthy subjects** across 1001 phase points (source: main_cohort_excel), pooling both body sides and all ages. It is not matched to this participant. Critically, the healthy reference cohort's self-selected speeds span 0.80–1.79 m/s, and this subject walks at 0.32 m/s — **well below that floor, so no speed-matched healthy reference exists in this dataset.** Every deviation reported in Sections 2 and 3 therefore conflates slow-walking effects with any stroke-related control changes, and these two contributions cannot be separated from the supplied data. Deviations should be read as differences from that specific empirical reference, not as measures of disease effect, severity, or clinical diagnosis. Group membership in the stroke cohort likewise does not, by itself, establish that any single listed value is caused by stroke.

**Channels and fields available / not available.** Sagittal-plane kinematics are supplied for ankle, knee, hip and pelvis; coronal and transverse data are not included in this input. EMG mean-envelope metrics are supplied for GAS, RF, VL, BF, ST, TA and ERS. Not supplied in this task: individual toe-off and other gait events, matched muscle-specific normal timing references, stride-level variability, raw-signal quality checks, expert plots, age, sex, time post-stroke, stroke clinical scores, and any non-paretic-side comparison. These are absent **from this input**, which is not the same as documented absent recordings in the source release.

## 2. EMG Envelope and Threshold Findings

**How these numbers must be read.** The curves are ensemble-averaged envelopes. Normalization uses a shared maximum across available strides before averaging (participant-, side-, and muscle-specific in the checked stroke release; not shared between sides), with no further amplitude normalization in this pipeline. Consequently: amplitudes are **relative envelope values**, not neural activation, force or strength; muscles must not be ranked as stronger or weaker from these values; and an averaged curve cannot establish stride-to-stride variability or that the same pattern occurred in every stride. "Above-threshold interval" means the region where the mean envelope exceeds 20% of **its own** peak, with a minimum retained interval of 3% of the cycle by sample count. An onset at 0% is the analysis-window boundary, not a physiological onset. Intervals written with X > Y wrap the 100%→0% boundary, i.e. the mean envelope remains above threshold across the cycle-defining event. These intervals are properties of the averaged envelope under this threshold-and-duration rule; they are not physiological onset, offset, contraction duration, or evidence of inactivity outside the intervals.

Descriptively, six of seven channels show a retained interval continuous across the cycle-defining event, with high single-interval coverage (RF 95%, BF 92%, VL 86%, ST 77%). GAS is the only channel with a single bounded interval not crossing the boundary (52–79%, 28% coverage). TA is fragmented into six retained intervals totalling 64% coverage, including short retained segments in the 21–49% region and a longer 74–92% segment plus a boundary-crossing 95–19% segment. ERS is reported as above threshold for the entire cycle (0–100%, 100%): here the supplied evidence does establish that no segment of this mean envelope fell below its own 20% threshold. For the other channels, coverage values below 100% indicate that below-threshold portions of the mean envelope exist, but rounded coverage alone would not license a statement that a given muscle had *no* below-threshold segment. Conversely, the gaps between TA's retained intervals reflect the mean envelope falling below threshold or a candidate region failing the 3%-duration rule — they do not demonstrate muscular silence.

Anatomically these channels correspond to ankle plantarflexion (GAS), ankle dorsiflexion (TA), knee extension (RF, VL), knee flexion / hip extension (BF, ST) and trunk/lumbar extension (ERS). No interval is mapped here to a functional task such as clearance, push-off or weight acceptance, and no interval is characterized as normal, early, delayed or prolonged, because individual toe-off and matched muscle-specific timing references are not supplied.

**Max |z| interpretation.** Max |z| is an extreme-value statistic — the largest absolute standardized difference across all 1001 phase points against the pooled reference mean/SD at each phase. Its location marks the largest standardized difference, **not** necessarily the time of peak envelope amplitude and not a gait event. "Above reference" means above the phase-specific reference mean at that location, not above a healthy percentile limit. The conventional 2-SD rule does not apply: in this dataset all 109 healthy controls exceed |z| = 2 on at least one muscle, and per-muscle healthy 95th percentiles for this statistic run 3.65 (ERS) to 4.61 (TA), higher still under leave-one-out. Against those per-muscle healthy ranges, TA's 3.8 at 86% sits between the ERS-level and TA-level 95th percentiles and is below the TA 95th percentile of 4.61; RF's 4.8 at 28% exceeds the highest quoted per-muscle 95th percentile value; and GAS 19.7, BF 8.8, ST 8.7, VL 7.3 and ERS 7.0 are numerically well above the quoted percentile range. These are descriptive magnitudes relative to an unmatched, non-speed-matched reference; they do not by themselves establish disease, abnormal physiology, or a clinical grade. Note also that pooled calibration percentiles are not matched leave-one-out normal limits.

**Co-contraction indices.** CCI is the Falconer–Winter index computed on the full continuous envelopes (2 × common area / summed areas) over the whole cycle. It is an overlap index of normalized envelopes — not simultaneous activation time, not muscular force, and not phase-specific. Because sub-threshold envelope area contributes, CCI can be non-zero even when detected bursts do not overlap.
- **TA vs GAS = 0.49**, inside the supplied healthy interval 0.24–0.59 and above the supplied median of 0.39.
- **RF vs BF = 0.78**, inside the supplied healthy interval 0.48–0.83 (nearer its upper endpoint) and above the supplied median of 0.68.

Falling inside these intervals does not establish physiologically normal co-activation or normal muscle function, and a value outside them would not establish pathological co-activation, stiffness, impaired motor control or any diagnosis. Both values depend on the normalization and envelope methods used here and must not be compared with thresholds or values from other studies or algorithms.

ROM values are reported as measured excursions. A magnitude above a pooled percentile therefore cannot be attributed wholly, or in any quantified proportion, to stroke pathology; slow-speed effects and stroke-related control differences are inseparable in these data. The same interpretive limits would apply to a control participant compared against this reference.

## 4. Questions for Further Clinical Assessment

The following are open questions for the referring physical therapist or physiatrist. None presupposes that the mechanism or measurement issue in question has been observed; each asks whether it exists.

1. **Event data.** Are individual gait events — particularly paretic toe-off — available in the source release for this participant? If so, can the retained envelope intervals be re-expressed relative to those events, which is a prerequisite for any timing judgment (including whether a pattern is early, delayed or prolonged)?
2. **Speed-comparable reference.** Is a speed-comparable or otherwise matched reference (e.g. slow-walking controls, or the participant's own non-paretic side with its side-specific normalization caveat) obtainable, so that the large composite and max |z| magnitudes can be examined against a comparison less confounded by a 0.32 m/s walking speed?
3. **Signal quality.** Have raw-signal quality checks been performed for these channels? In particular, is there any evidence of cross-talk, baseline offset, motion artifact or low signal-to-noise that could account for the near-continuous above-threshold coverage in RF, BF, VL, ST and especially ERS (100%), or for the fragmentation of the TA envelope into six retained intervals?
4. **Normalization denominator.** Can the participant-, side- and muscle-specific normalization maxima be inspected? A low or artifact-driven denominator would alter both the relative amplitudes and the 20%-of-own-peak interval boundaries, and this cannot be assessed from mean curves alone.
5. **Stride-level behaviour.** Are individual-stride envelopes retrievable? The supplied averages cannot show whether the boundary-crossing coverage and the TA fragmentation are consistent across strides or driven by a subset of strides.
6. **Step-length definition sensitivity.** Is the instantaneous-definition symmetry value also available for this participant, to establish whether the −11.3% (shorter paretic step) is robust to the operational definition?
7. **Out-of-plane motion.** Are coronal and transverse kinematics recorded in the release? The present composite reflects sagittal channels only, so any out-of-plane contribution is unrepresented here.
8. **Clinical context.** Are cohort-applicable fields such as time post-stroke and stroke clinical scores available for this participant, and can standing/passive sagittal range and a clinical examination be used to interpret the reported ROM values, which have no supplied reference for comparison?
